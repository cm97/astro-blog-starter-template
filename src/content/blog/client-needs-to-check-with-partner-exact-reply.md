They liked the proposal. Then: “I need to run this by my partner / team / accountant.”

That is not a yes. It is also not a no. It is the sentence that parks the deal inside someone else’s calendar while your start date sits open and your brain keeps checking the inbox.

You do not need a better proposal. You need one short reply that names a date and a binary, plus a fixed next step so it does not live in your head.

## The 3-minute log the second they say it

Copy this before you reply. Blank fields mean the follow-up does not exist.

```
Name: ________
Proposal date: ________
Who they need to check with: ________
Price you named: $________
Start date you offered: ________
Reply due: ________ (today + 4 days)
Status: waiting / replied / closed
Next action: ________
```

Two minutes. Do it while the message is still open. Tomorrow erases the exact words.

## The exact reply (send within 2 hours)

Subject: Re: [Project name]

> Got it. To keep the [start date] open I need a yes or no by [day + 4].
>
> Reply “yes” and I send the invoice + the one thing I need from you.
>
> Reply “later” and I release the date and note to check in [month they named].
>
> Either way is fine. Just need the word so it does not sit open while you check.

One paragraph. Binary. Dated. No “let me know what they say.”

## Extra concrete steps that force the decision

1. Send the reply within 2 hours of their message. After that the window cools and the date loses weight.
2. Put the reply-due date on your calendar as an 8-minute block titled “Close [Name] partner-check.” If it is not on the calendar it dies.
3. Fill the log the same minute. Screenshot the original message into the note. Memory of the exact words is not reliable on Thursday.
4. If they named a specific person or concern, restate it in one line above the binary close so they see you heard it. Do not add a new paragraph of selling.
5. After you send, set a phone reminder for the due date morning. Calendar alone gets ignored when delivery is on fire.
6. Cap open “partner-check” rows at three. A fourth means you are collecting maybes instead of closing. Close or park the oldest.
7. If the start date you named has already passed by the reply-due day, replace it with the next date you can actually hold before you send the close email.
8. On the reply-due morning, open the log first. If status is still waiting, send the closing email before 11am. Do not wait for a “better” feeling.
9. After any yes, invoice the same hour and add one line to next Monday’s onboarding sheet: “[Name] — day-2 intake or kickoff due.” The follow-up ends when money lands; onboarding starts.
10. At the next weekly reset, add a row: “Open partner-checks past reply-due.” If none, write none.

Fill this after every send:

```
Reply sent: yes / no — time: ________
Reply-due date: ________
Log filled: yes / no
Calendar block set: yes / no
```

## If they go quiet after that reply

Send this on the date you named, before 11am, mid-week only.

Subject: Closing the file on [Project name]

> Closing the loop. Releasing the [start date] unless you reply “yes” by end of day.
>
> If timing is off, reply “later” and I’ll note the month.

Then stop. No fourth email.

## What you do when they reply

- Yes: invoice the same hour. Welcome note with start date and the one asset you need. Add them to next Monday’s onboarding row.
- Later: write the month. One note that month. Status = parked.
- Price or scope question: restate what is in and out. Do not cut the number. Restate the binary close in the same reply.
- No: mark closed. Do not argue.

## Why “I need to check with my partner” dies without this

The sentence lets them leave the decision open forever while someone else holds the file. Your reply turns it into a yes, a later, or a clean no. The date keeps it out of your head so Thursday delivery does not erase the follow-up.
