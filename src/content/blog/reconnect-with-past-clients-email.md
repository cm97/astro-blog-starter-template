The project wrapped. The client was happy. You meant to check in after a month. Now it has been four, and every week it feels more awkward to send anything.

So you don't. And the easiest work you will ever win sits in your sent folder, untouched.

A past client already knows you, already trusts you, and has already paid you once. You do not need a pitch. You need one short email and a date to send it.

## Why the check-in never gets sent

It is not that you forgot the client. It is that the check-in has no due date.

Active work has deadlines. Proposals have follow-up days. A finished client has nothing. So the check-in lives in your head next to "update the website," and it loses every week.

The fix is to give every finished client a date the day the project ends.

## The 2-minute log when a project wraps

Fill this in the same day you send the final invoice.

```
Client: ________
What we did: ________ (one line)
Result they mentioned: ________ (their words, if any)
Wrapped: ________ (date)
Check-in 1 (6 weeks): ________
Check-in 2 (4 months): ________
Status: active / check-in due / replied / not a fit
```

Two minutes. Blank dates mean the check-in does not exist.

## Version 1 — The 6-week check-in

Send this about six weeks after the work ends. It asks about their result, not your next sale.

Subject: How is [the thing we did] going?

> Hi [Name],
>
> It has been about six weeks since we wrapped [project]. How is [the specific thing] working out?
>
> No agenda. I just like to know how it holds up once I'm out of the picture.
>
> [Your name]

Send it mid-week, before 11am. Log it. Move on.

## Version 2 — The "it's been a while" check-in

For the client you meant to email months ago. Name the gap once and move past it.

Subject: Checking in on [project]

> Hi [Name],
>
> I was looking back at [project] and realized it has been a while. I hope [their business / the launch / the team] is going well.
>
> If anything has come up since then that I could help with, I'm happy to hear about it. If not, no reply needed.
>
> [Your name]

The last line matters. "No reply needed" takes the pressure off, and people reply more when they don't have to.

## Version 3 — The "I have room" check-in

Use this when you have open capacity and want work. Be direct about it.

Subject: Opening for [month]

> Hi [Name],
>
> I have room for one or two projects starting [month]. You came to mind first because [project] went well.
>
> If you have anything on the list, like [a likely next step for them], I'd be glad to talk it through. If the timing is wrong, no problem at all.
>
> [Your name]

Send this one to past clients only, not cold leads. It works because they already know your work.

## What not to send

- **"Just checking in!"** with nothing else. It gives them nothing to reply to.
- **A newsletter instead of a note.** A past client gets a personal email, not a blast.
- **A discount.** It tells them your price was negotiable all along.
- **A long update about your business.** The email is about them.

## What to do when they reply

- **They have work:** reply the same day with a short call link or a two-line scope question. Use your normal proposal process.
- **They say all is well:** thank them in one line. Set the next check-in four months out.
- **They mention a friend or colleague:** ask if they're happy to introduce you. One sentence, no pressure.
- **No reply:** mark it sent. The next check-in date stays on the log. Do not send a second email this month.

## Make it a Monday habit, not a guilt pile

Inside your weekly reset, add one line: "Past clients due this week."

1. Open the log.
2. Find every check-in date that has passed.
3. Send at most two this week. The rest wait for next Monday.
4. Write the next date on each row you send.

Ten minutes a week. No client goes quiet because you were busy.
