The message lands while you are already in delivery: "Can we jump on a quick call?"

It sounds short. It never is. Twenty minutes of calendar, ten minutes of prep, then the call runs long because the actual question was still in their head. Your afternoon block is gone and the next client still needs the file.

This is the reply that answers the need without opening the calendar. You send it once. The work stays async. The hour stays yours.

## The 60-second log before you reply

Open the same note you use for the Monday scan. Add the row.

```
Name: ________
Project: ________
Ask received: ________
What they actually need (best guess): ________
Reply sent: ________
Status: async / scheduled / closed
```

If you cannot name the real need in one line, the call will invent one. Force the one-line version first.

## Exact reply that keeps it async

Send within two hours. Subject: Re: [Project name] — quick question

> Hi [Name],
>
> Happy to get you unstuck. Drop the exact question or the one decision you need here and I will reply with the clear next step today. That keeps both of us moving without losing an hour to calendar.
>
> If it really needs a call after that, we can put 15 minutes on the calendar tomorrow morning. Otherwise the email is faster.
>
> [Your name]

One short block. No apology. No "sure, when works?" The default is the written answer.

Do not open your calendar link in the first reply. Do not ask them to pick a time.

## What you do when they reply (or don't)

- They send the actual question: answer it in the same thread the same day. Log status as async. Move on.
- They insist on a call anyway: offer one 15-minute slot the next morning only. Cap it. End at 15. Do not invent a second slot.
- Silence after this reply: wait 2 full days, then send one line that restates the written option. Do not chase with a second calendar offer.
- They send a novel instead of one question: reply with the single next action only. Do not solve the whole project in email.

## Timing that protects the week

- Reply the same day the ask arrives. Delay turns a quick question into a meeting request.
- Weekday hours only. Never after 6pm or on Friday afternoon.
- Cap open "needs call" rows at two. More than two means the written-first rule is slipping.
- Past clients get the same words. Warm does not mean free calendar time.

## Extra concrete steps that stop the calendar leak

1. Write the one-line need before you hit send. If you cannot, the reply is not ready.
2. Keep this template in the same note as the Monday scan and the scope replies. One open, all available.
3. After you send the reply, move the row out of "active ask" the same hour. The scan only shows live items.
4. If three of these asks land in the same week, look at the last status update you sent. Vague "let me know if you want to talk" language creates the ask.
5. Never open the calendar link until they have answered the written question. Memory of "they seemed stuck" is not a reason to book.
6. At the weekly reset, count how many "quick call" replies you sent that stayed async. Zero is a signal you are still saying yes in the moment.
7. If the real need is a decision that lives in the original proposal, paste the relevant line so the answer is already in the thread.
8. Default to the reply above when you feel the pull to be helpful. The exact boundary matters more than the feeling.

## Fill-in after every send

```
Reply sent: ________ (date/time)
Call status: avoided / scheduled / done
Status logged: async / scheduled / closed
Next action: none / 2-day nudge / close
```

## Why this works when "sure, hop on" fails

Saying yes to every quick call trains the client that your calendar is the support channel and trains you that delivery hours are optional. The reply frees the mental slot for the actual work and gives them a faster path if the need is real. Most "quick calls" die once the question is written down.
