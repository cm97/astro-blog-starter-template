Your proposal said two rounds of changes. You've done two rounds. Then the email lands: "Love it! Just a few more tweaks."

The tweaks are a new headline, a different colour, and "can we try the other layout again?" That's a third round. Maybe a fourth after it.

You don't want to sound petty over a colour. So you do it for free. Then the next one. The project that was priced for two rounds quietly turns into five, and the final invoice stays the same.

You need one short reply that is friendly, names the limit, and hands the client a choice.

## First: check what you actually agreed

Open the proposal or contract before you write anything. Fill this in:

```
Client: ________
Rounds included (from the proposal): ________
Rounds used so far: ________ (dates)
What counts as a round (one list of changes / one call): ________
New request is: same round / new round / new scope
```

Two things to watch:

- **If the proposal doesn't mention rounds,** you can't enforce a limit you never set. Do this round, and add the limit to every proposal from now on (the line is at the end of this post).
- **If the new request is a new feature,** not a change to existing work, it isn't a revision at all. Use the [scope reply](/blog/can-you-just-add-this-exact-reply) instead.

## The reply

Send it in the same thread, within a day of their request. Don't start the changes first.

> Subject: Re: [project] revisions
>
> Hi [name],
>
> Glad it's close. The proposal for [project] includes [2] rounds of changes, and we've used them ([date] and [date]).
>
> Two ways to go from here:
>
> 1. I do this round for [price], back to you by [date].
> 2. We lock in the current version and I send the final files.
>
> Which would you prefer?
>
> [Your name]

Short. No apology. No list of everything you've already done for them.

## Why this works

- **It points at the proposal, not at you.** "The proposal includes 2 rounds" is a fact you both agreed. It's not you being difficult.
- **The dates make it concrete.** Clients often lose count of rounds. Two dates settle it without an argument.
- **Both options are a yes.** Paying for the round is a yes. Locking the version is also a yes, and the project ends. Neither one is "no".
- **It goes out before you start.** Once you've done the work, asking to be paid for it feels like a surprise bill.

## Pricing the extra round

Pick the number before you send, so you're not guessing in the email.

- **Simple rule:** your hourly rate times the hours you think the round will take, rounded up to a whole number.
- **Or a flat fee per round,** written in the proposal for next time. A flat fee is easier for clients to say yes to.

If you don't know your hourly rate, the [pricing post](/blog/pricing-your-time-without-guessing) walks through it.

## When the client pushes back

**"I thought small changes were included."**

> Small fixes are always included: typos, broken links, anything that's not how we agreed. These changes are new direction, so they'd be a new round. Happy to do it, or to lock in what we have.

**"The last round didn't get it right, so it shouldn't count."**

If they're right, and you missed something they clearly asked for, fix that piece for free. Then say:

> Fixed [specific item] at no charge, since that was in the last round's notes. The other changes would be a new round at [price].

**"Can you just do this one, as a favour?"**

You can. If you do, say so in writing so it doesn't become the new normal:

> I'll include this one as a one-off. Any further changes after this would be [price] per round.

**They go quiet.**

Wait 3 working days, then one line in the same thread:

> Hi [name], should I lock in the current version and send the final files?

## Stop it next time: one line in your proposal

Add this to the "What's included" part of every proposal:

> Includes [2] rounds of changes. A round is one list of changes sent together. Extra rounds are [price] each.

"One list sent together" matters. Without it, ten separate emails can count as one round.
