It's 10:14pm. Your phone lights up. "Quick question about the homepage."

You answer, because you don't want to seem slow. Now the client knows 10pm works. Next week it's 11pm, then Sunday morning.

The client isn't being rude. They don't know your hours, because you never told them. One short email fixes that, and most clients are relieved to get it.

## Why answering fast makes it worse

Every late reply teaches the client that you're always on. They stop batching questions. Small things that could wait for one daily email turn into ten texts.

You end up working all day in pieces, and the real work takes longer. The next Monday reset has no clean start because last night's 10pm reply is still in your head.

## Before you send it

Decide three things first. It takes five minutes.

1. **Your hours.** For example, Monday to Friday, 9 to 5. Pick what you'll actually keep.
2. **Your reply time.** "Within one business day" is normal and fair.
3. **What counts as urgent, and how to reach you for it.** For most projects, nothing is truly urgent. If something is, give one channel for it.

```
Hours: ________
Reply within: ________
Urgent means: ________
Urgent channel: ________
Project name: ________
Client first name: ________
```

Write the answers once. Copy them into the email. Do not invent new hours for each client.

## The email

Subject: **How I'll keep [project] moving**

> Hi [first name],
>
> Quick note on how I work, so you always know when you'll hear back.
>
> I'm working on client projects [days], [hours]. Messages sent outside those hours get a reply the next working morning.
>
> If something is truly urgent, like [example of urgent], [how to reach you].
>
> Batching questions into one email a day helps me keep [project] on schedule.
>
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

It's short and friendly, and it's about the project, not about you being tired. Time box: two minutes to fill the blanks and send.

## The next late message

The email only works if your behavior matches it. When the next 10pm message comes in, don't answer it that night.

Reply the next morning:

> Morning [first name], thanks for this. Here's the answer: [answer].

No apology for the delay. The delay was the plan.

If you must reply that night, schedule the email to send at 9am. Do not open the app after hours.

## When they push back

**"It's just a quick question."**

> Happy to answer it. I'll reply first thing tomorrow so it's in one place with anything else that comes up.

**"I need it sorted before my meeting."**

> Understood. For anything with a deadline, let me know a day ahead and I'll make sure it's ready.

**"Other freelancers answer at night."**

> I keep set hours so I can give [project] my full attention. You'll always hear back within one working day.

One sentence each. No long explanations. Paste, send, log the time.

## Extra steps that keep the boundary

- Send the boundary email the same day as kickoff, before any late message arrives. Put the hours in the kickoff summary next to scope and dates.
- Log the send time next to the client's onboarding checklist. If it is only in the thread, it vanishes when you switch projects.
- Set a calendar hold labeled "Reply window ends" for 5pm. Anything after that waits until morning. Unscheduled replies do not leave.
- On the next Monday reset, scan for any client who messaged outside hours last week. One line: "Boundary held or slipped?" If slipped, resend the email and note the new date.
- Never rewrite the hours for one client. One set of hours for every project. Exceptions are how the boundary dies.
- If a true urgent lands, use the channel you named, answer the one thing, then close the app. Do not open the rest of the inbox.
- After the first successful morning reply, the client usually stops testing. The second late message is rarer. The third almost never arrives.
