You spent four hours on a proposal. Twelve pages. Brand colors. A cover letter. They read the first paragraph and replied: "Can you just send a price?"

That's because proposals are not for you. They're for the person who has to justify the spend to someone else. And that person does not want a novel. They want three things: what you are doing, what it costs, and what happens if they say yes.

## The one-page structure

**Line 1: The outcome.** Not "I will build you a website." "You will have a site that books clients while you sleep." Outcome first. Always.

**Line 2: The scope.** Three bullets max. What is in. What is not. Be brutal about the not — that's where scope creep lives.

**Line 3: The price.** One number. No ranges. No "starting at." One number.

**Line 4: The timeline.** When it starts. When it ends. What you need from them to hit that date.

**Line 5: The next step.** "Reply yes and I send the invoice." Not "let me know your thoughts." A decision, not a vibe check.

## The fill-in template

**Subject:** Proposal for [project name]

> Hi [Name],
>
> You mentioned [their problem, in their words]. Here is how I would solve it: [2–3 sentences, no jargon].
>
> **Scope:**
> - [Specific deliverable 1]
> - [Specific deliverable 2]
> - [Specific deliverable 3]
> - Not included: [the thing they will assume is included]
>
> **Investment:** $[amount], due [terms].
>
> **Timeline:** starts [date], [milestone] by [date], final delivery [date]. I need [what you need from them] by [date] to hit that.
>
> **Next step:** reply "yes" and I will send the invoice.
>
> [Your name]

## Why this works

It respects their time. It forces you to be clear. And it gives the person who has to approve the spend something they can forward in one email.
