You spent four hours on a proposal. Twelve pages. Brand colors. A cover letter. They read the first paragraph and replied: "Can you just send a price?"

Or they did not reply at all. The proposal sits in sent mail. You rewrite it in your head instead of sending the same five lines on day 6.

That is not a writing problem. It is a missing sequence. A proposal that closes is one page, one number, and two already-written follow-ups.

## The one-page structure

Five lines. If it needs a sixth, cut something.

1. **Outcome.** Not "I will build you a website." "You will have a site that books clients while you sleep." Their result, not your task list.
2. **Scope.** Three bullets in. One bullet not included. The "not included" line is where scope creep starts if you leave it blank.
3. **Price.** One number. No ranges. No "starting at."
4. **Timeline.** Start date, end date, and the one thing you need from them to hit it.
5. **Next step.** "Reply yes and I send the invoice." Not "let me know your thoughts."

Time box: 20 minutes. If the draft is still open after that, you are decorating, not proposing.

## Fill-in proposal

Subject: Proposal for [project name]

> Hi [Name],
>
> You mentioned [their problem, in their words]. Here is how I would solve it: [2–3 sentences, no jargon].
>
> **Scope:**
> - [Specific deliverable 1]
> - [Specific deliverable 2]
> - [Specific deliverable 3]
> - Not included: [the thing they will assume is included]
>
> **Investment:** $[amount], due [terms].
>
> **Timeline:** starts [date], [milestone] by [date], final delivery [date]. I need [what you need from them] by [date] to hit that.
>
> **Next step:** reply "yes" and I will send the invoice.
>
> [Your name]

Log it the same minute you hit send:

```
Name: ________
Sent: ________
Price: ________
Day-3 follow-up date: ________
Day-6 follow-up date: ________
Status: waiting / yes / no / parked
```

If the dates are not written, the follow-up dies the first slammed Thursday.

## Day 3 — one bump, same thread

Do not send a new novel. Reply on the original email.

> Checking this landed. Same offer: [one-line outcome] at $[amount]. Reply yes and I send the invoice, or no and I close the file.

One question. An easy out. No "just circling back" and no new discount.

## Day 6 — the close-the-file note

Still no reply. Same thread. Last touch unless they write back.

> Closing this on my side Friday unless I hear otherwise. Offer still stands at $[amount] if you want it later — reply yes and I reopen the file.

Then mark the row parked. Do not rewrite the proposal. Do not add a third follow-up this week.

If they reply "not now":

> Got it. I will check once on [date 30 days out]. One line is enough if the timing changes before then.

Put that date on the same row. That is the whole quiet-lead path.

## What still breaks when it only lives in your head

- The proposal becomes twelve pages because there is no line limit.
- Day 3 and day 6 are not on a calendar, so the quiet lead never gets the same five lines.
- You invent a new discount on day 6 because the original number was not written next to the send date.
- A yes has nowhere to land, so onboarding starts from memory the morning they pay.
