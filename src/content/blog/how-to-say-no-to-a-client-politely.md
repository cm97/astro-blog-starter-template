Saying no to a client feels risky when you work alone. Every client matters, so it's tempting to say yes and sort it out later. Later is usually a weekend.

A good no isn't a rejection. It's a clear answer, given kindly and quickly, that tells the client what you *can* do instead. Most clients respect it more than a reluctant yes.

Here are five situations and the exact words for each.

## 1. "Can you just add this?"

The request is small, friendly and outside the agreed scope.

> Happy to help with that. It's outside what we agreed for this project, so I'd quote it separately: [price], ready by [date]. Or we can swap it for [planned item] and keep the budget the same. Which would you prefer?

You haven't refused. You've put a price and a choice on it. More on this in [the exact reply to "can you just add this?"](/blog/can-you-just-add-this-exact-reply/).

## 2. A deadline you can't meet

> I want to get this right, and [date] doesn't leave enough time to do that. I can deliver by [realistic date], or by [their date] if we drop [item] from this round. Which works better for you?

Offer two real options. Never agree to a date you already know you'll miss.

## 3. A discount request

> I understand budgets are tight. I keep my rates the same for every client, but I can reduce the scope to fit [their budget]: [smaller option]. Would that work?

Lower the scope, not the price. If you discount once, it becomes the new price.

## 4. Calls and messages after hours

> Thanks for the message. I'm offline in the evenings so I can give your project my full attention during the day. I'll reply first thing tomorrow.

Then actually reply first thing tomorrow. The boundary holds because the client learns you're reliable inside it. There's a longer version in [the after-hours boundary email](/blog/client-messages-after-hours-boundary-email/).

## 5. A project that isn't a fit

> Thank you for thinking of me. This one isn't a good fit for what I do best, and I'd rather tell you now than do a half job. [Name or type of specialist] would be a better match.

Saying no to the wrong project leaves room for the right one, and a referral keeps the goodwill.

## Three rules that make any no easier

- **Answer fast.** A quick no is kinder than a slow maybe.
- **Give one reason, not five.** Long explanations sound like guilt and invite negotiation.
- **Always offer a next step.** A price, a later date, a smaller version or a referral.
