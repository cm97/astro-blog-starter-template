The project is agreed. Then the agency's onboarding email arrives: "Please note our standard payment terms are net 60 from invoice receipt."

Sixty days after you invoice. Which is after you deliver. So work you start this week gets paid in about three months. And "net 60" on paper often turns into 75 or 90 days in practice.

Most freelancers just accept it, because the client is bigger and arguing feels risky. You don't have to argue. You do have to answer **before you start**, because once the work is done you have nothing left to negotiate with.

## First: find out if the terms are fixed

Big companies often have a standard term in their system. Sometimes it's truly fixed. Often the person emailing you just doesn't know there are exceptions.

Fill this in before you reply:

```
Client: ________
Their terms: net ___ from ___ (invoice date / receipt / approval)
Project size: ________
How long the work takes: ________
Total wait from first day of work to payment: ________ days
Could you cover your costs for that long? yes / no
```

That last line is your real answer. If waiting 90 days would hurt, you need one of the options below.

## The reply

Send it in the same thread, before any work starts.

> Hi [name],
>
> Thanks for sending this over. Before I start, one thing on payment.
>
> My standard terms are 50% at the start and 50% on delivery, due within 14 days. For net 60, I'd need one of these:
>
> 1. A 50% deposit before I start, with the rest on net 60, or
> 2. Net 30 on the full invoice, or
> 3. Net 60 on the full amount at [price + 10%] to cover the longer wait.
>
> Happy with whichever is easiest on your side. Could you check which one your finance team can do?
>
> [Your name]

Three options, all reasonable, all a yes. It goes to finance, which is the right place, because the person you're working with often has no say over payment terms.

## Why this works

- **It goes out before you start.** Your leverage is the work you haven't done yet. After delivery, there's none left.
- **It doesn't say no.** It says "yes, on one of these terms". Clients rarely walk away over a deposit.
- **A deposit is normal.** Many agencies can pay a deposit or a "mobilisation" invoice. They just don't offer it unless asked.
- **Option 3 puts a price on the wait.** If they truly can't move, at least the long wait is paid for. Pick a number you'd be happy with. 10% is a starting point, not a rule.

## When they say "net 60 is the only option"

Sometimes it really is fixed. You still have choices that don't need their finance team:

**Split the project into smaller invoices.**

> Understood. Could we invoice in stages instead? [Stage 1] on [date], [Stage 2] on [date], final on delivery. Each on net 60.

Smaller invoices that start earlier mean money arrives sooner, even on the same terms.

**Ask when the clock starts.**

"Net 60 from invoice date" is very different from "net 60 from approval". Ask:

> Does the 60 days start from the invoice date, or from when it's approved on your side?

If it's from approval, ask who approves and how long that usually takes. Then invoice the same day you deliver, not at the end of the month.

**Get the details now, not on day 61.**

> What do you need on the invoice so it goes straight through? PO number, cost code, the name of the approver?

Most late payments from big clients are invoices stuck in a queue because one field was missing. Ask once, write the answers down, and put them on every invoice.

## Write it down

Whatever you agree, confirm it in one line before you start:

> Great. To confirm: [50% deposit before start, balance on net 60 from invoice date]. I'll send the deposit invoice today, and I'll start once it's paid.

Then don't start until the deposit lands. If they start pushing for work before it's paid, there's a [reply for that too](/blog/client-asks-to-start-before-deposit-exact-reply).

## When the due date passes

Put three reminders on your calendar the day you send the invoice: 1 day, 7 days and 14 days after the due date. That way you're not deciding whether to chase. You just do it. The [unpaid invoice script](/blog/unpaid-invoice-follow-up-script) has the exact emails.

If you're dealing with a large unpaid amount, or a contract with late-payment terms, check the rules where you work. Some countries give you a legal right to interest on late business invoices. This post isn't legal advice.
