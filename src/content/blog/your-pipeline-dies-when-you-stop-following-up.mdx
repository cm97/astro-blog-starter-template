You had the call. You sent the proposal the same day. Then the thread went quiet and you treated silence like a no.

It was not a no. You stopped the sequence. Day 3 you opened a draft and closed it. Day 7 a client delivery ate the morning. Day 14 the lead is gone and you never got a decision. The discovery call, the scope notes, and the price you wrote are unpaid work.

This is not a prospecting problem. It is a follow-up problem. A busy week will always beat a feeling that says "I should check in." The fix is a cadence you send even when you are slammed.

## The rule

One proposal gets three touches after the send. Same days. Same length. Then you close the file.

Do not write a new pitch on each touch. Do not add a discount. Do not wait for a calm hour. If the date is on the calendar, the email goes out.

## Before you send the proposal (2 minutes)

Write the follow-up dates while the call is still open. If they are not on the calendar, they will not happen.

```
Proposal sent: ________
Day 3 bump: ________
Day 7 value line: ________
Day 14 close or clear: ________
Start date you are holding: ________
```

Name one start date you can actually hold. "Hold the start date or release it" is the whole close. A floating "let me know" gives them nothing to answer.

## Day 3 — light bump (5 lines)

No new offer. No attachment. One question.

Subject: Start date still open

> Hi [Name] — sent the proposal on [day].
> Still holding [start date] for you.
> One question if useful: is the blocker price, timing, or scope?
> One line is enough.
> If I do not hear back I will bump this once more on [day 7].

Send it. Do not rewrite the subject line four times.

## Day 7 — one useful line, then the ask

Not a new PDF. One observation from the call, then the same date.

Subject: One thing from our call

> Hi [Name] — the part that was costing you time was [one specific thing they said].
> The proposal covers that and nothing else.
> I can still start [date]. After [day 14] I release that slot.
> Want me to hold it?

If they reply with a real objection, answer that objection in one paragraph and restate the date. Do not open a second proposal.

## Day 14 — close or clear

People answer a breakup more often than a fifth nudge.

Subject: Closing the file

> Hi [Name] — I am releasing the [date] start.
> If the timing changes, reply to this thread and I will send the same scope with a new date.
> No need to explain. File is closed on my side unless you say otherwise.

Then stop. A closed file is a result. An open file you never touch is how the pipeline dies.

## What to do with the reply

- Yes: invoice the same day. Send the welcome note with the start date, the one thing you need from them, and the day they will hear from you next.
- Not now: log the month they named. One reminder that month. Not a weekly drip.
- Price: restate what is in and what is out. Do not cut the number to "get a win."
- Scope change: new line, new price, new date. The original proposal stays the record.

If you cannot name the next date after a yes, you do not have a client yet. You have a payment waiting on a process that still lives in your head.

## Wednesday tie-in (5 minutes)

On the weekly reset, every open proposal is one line: last touch, next touch, date you are holding. If the next touch is blank, it is overdue. Send it in that block. Do not leave it for "after delivery."

```
Lead: ________
Last touch: ________
Next touch: day 3 / 7 / 14 on ________
Date held: ________
Status: open / closed
```

## What still breaks if this only lives in your head

The day-3 note sits in drafts. The day-7 note waits for a clever insight. The day-14 close never happens, so the lead looks alive and is not. Monday fills with delivery and the person who already heard the price gets leftover attention.
