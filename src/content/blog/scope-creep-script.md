Scope creep is not a client problem. It is a response problem.

The client asks for one small thing outside the agreement. You say "sure, no problem" because the ask seems small and saying no feels awkward. A week later there is another small ask. Then another.

Six weeks in you have done four extra hours of work for free and resent the client for it. The client has no idea. That gap is where projects go sideways and invoices get argued.

## Why "that's not in scope" fails

Telling a client something is out of scope without a path forward creates conflict. They do not know what to do with that information. They pushed on the boundary and you gave them nothing to push toward.

The answer is not a harder no. It is a structure that gives them two options, both of which protect your time.

## Run this before you answer yes

Do this in order. Do not skip the pause.

1. **Do not answer in the same message.** Reply with: "Got it. Let me check what we already locked and I will come back in an hour." That sentence is the whole first move.
2. **Open the original scope.** Pull the signed note, the kickoff email, or the onboarding checklist. Highlight the line that covers this request, or the line that does not.
3. **Name the extra work in minutes, not vibes.** "This is about 90 minutes of extra work" beats "this is a bigger project now."
4. **Put a price on those minutes.** Use your existing rate. If you do not have one written down, use the number you already charge for a similar add-on. Write it before you type the client email.
5. **Send two options only.** Add it as a paid line item, or park it until this project wraps. No third option of "I will just squeeze it in."
6. **Wait for a written yes.** Do not start the extra work on a thumbs-up in a voice note. One sentence back: "Reply YES and I will add the line item."
7. **Log the decision in the same place you log weekly work.** If it only lives in the thread, next Thursday you will forget you already said no or already billed it.

Time box for the whole loop: 20 minutes. If it takes longer, you are rewriting the project instead of pricing the add-on.

## The script

When they ask for something outside scope:

> "I can do that. It is outside what we agreed on, so it is a separate line item. Here is what it costs: [price]. Want me to add it, or should we keep it for after this project wraps?"

No apology. No "I would love to but." No rambling explanation. One sentence, one price, one choice.

If they push with "can't you just…?":

> "I can. Just not inside the current fee. Same two options: add it now at [price], or we park it."

If they say they thought it was included:

> "That piece was not in the original list. I can add it as a line item at [price], or we stay with what we already locked. Which do you want?"

If the brief changed mid-project:

> "This is a different project than the one we scoped. I can write a short addendum with the new deliverable and price, or we finish the original and start a new one after."

## Three rules that keep the script from collapsing

1. **Never say yes in the moment.** Buy the hour. "Let me check the original scope and get back to you."
2. **Always name the price.** Even if it is $25. Naming it resets the frame.
3. **Always give the out.** "If it is not worth it, we stick to the original scope."

## What still breaks if this only lives in your head

You will remember the script on a calm Tuesday. You will forget it on a slammed Thursday when the client texts during a delivery week. That is why the words have to sit next to onboarding and the weekly reset — same folder, same 20-minute run, not a speech you invent again.
