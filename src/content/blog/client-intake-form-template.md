Most client intake forms have two problems: they're too long, and they ask the wrong questions.

A twelve-question intake form that arrives in the prospect's inbox before they've even signed a contract signals that working with you will involve a lot of paperwork. A five-question form sent after the contract is signed — with a specific deadline and a clear explanation of why it matters — tells the client that you're organized, that you've thought about their time, and that their first call will be substantive rather than administrative.

Here's the intake form template that works for most coaching and consulting engagements, and the reasoning behind why each question is there.

## Why a client intake form matters

The alternative to a pre-call intake form is asking these questions on the call itself. That works, but it has costs:

**Time.** A 30-minute kickoff call that spends 15 minutes on basics that could have been collected in writing leaves 15 minutes for actual work. A kickoff call where you've already read the intake can start in the middle of the problem.

**Depth.** People give more considered answers in writing than they do in real-time conversation. A client who takes 20 minutes to write out their goals will give you a more honest, more detailed answer than the same client who answers off the top of their head on a call.

**Preparation.** Reviewing written intake answers before a call lets you identify the two or three things you most want to dig into, surface any red flags about scope or expectations, and arrive at the call with something to say rather than a list of questions to work through.

## The 5-question intake form template

These questions work for coaching, consulting, and most service-based engagements. Adapt the language for your specific practice.

---

**Question 1: What's the most important outcome you want from this engagement?**

*Not "what are your goals" — that's too broad. "Most important outcome" forces prioritization.*

Why it's there: Most clients have a list of things they want to improve. The intake form isn't the place to collect the whole list — that comes out over time. This question identifies the thing that, if it doesn't happen, they'll feel like the engagement didn't deliver. That's what you're anchoring the work to.

The answer to this question should show up explicitly in the kickoff call agenda. You're not starting the call by asking what they want — you're confirming what they told you.

---

**Question 2: What have you already tried that hasn't worked?**

*Context question. Prevents you from recommending what they've already rejected.*

Why it's there: This question does two things. First, it saves you from spending the first session discussing approaches the client has already tried and discarded. Second, it tells you what the client thinks they've tried — which often reveals misunderstandings about why something didn't work that become useful material in the engagement.

A client who answers "I've tried time-blocking but it always falls apart after a week" is telling you something specific about their situation. A client who answers "I don't know, I've tried a lot of things" is telling you something different.

---

**Question 3: Who else is involved in decisions about this area?**

*Stakeholder question. Surfaces organizational or personal constraints early.*

Why it's there: For business coaching or consulting, this question identifies whether you're working with someone who has full decision-making authority or someone who needs to get buy-in from a partner, a board, a spouse, or a team. Knowing this before the kickoff call means you can adjust how you frame recommendations.

For personal or life coaching, adapt this: "Is there anyone else in your life who will be affected by the changes you're working toward?" Same purpose — understanding the context around the person, not just the problem they've named.

---

**Question 4: What would make you feel, six months from now, that this was a great investment?**

*Outcome question. Establishes the real measure of success.*

Why it's there: This question asks the client to articulate success in their own terms. "Great investment" is a high bar — it's not "things improved slightly," it's "I feel clearly better off than I was." The answer tells you what the client is actually optimizing for, which is often different from what they say in the first conversation.

This question also creates a natural milestone: six months from now, you can ask them the same question and compare their answer to what they wrote in the intake. It's a built-in measure of progress.

---

**Question 5: Is there anything else I should know before we talk?**

*Open-field question. Catches what the other four didn't.*

Why it's there: Clients sometimes have context they want you to have but weren't sure was relevant to the other questions. A health situation that affects their schedule. A recent business decision that changes the priorities. A concern about the engagement they haven't voiced.

This question signals that there's space to share that kind of context. About 30% of clients will use it, and of those, most of the answers are useful.

## How to send the intake form

**Timing:** Send it within 24 hours of the contract being signed, with a 3-day deadline. Not 2 weeks before the kickoff — that gives clients too much time to keep pushing it. Not 24 hours before — that's not enough time for a thoughtful response.

**Format:** Send it as a link to a simple form (Google Forms, Typeform, Notion form — whatever you use). The form should not require an account, should not have branching logic, and should be completable on a phone in about 5 minutes.

**The covering message:**

> Hi [Name],
>
> Before our kickoff call, I'd like to send a short form with five questions. It takes about five minutes and helps me make the first session useful from the start rather than spending time on basics.
>
> [Link to form]
>
> Please return it by [date, 3 days out]. If anything comes up, you can reply here.
>
> [Your name]

**If the form doesn't come back:** Follow up once, by email, 48 hours before the deadline. "Just a reminder on the intake form — I want to review your answers before the call. Here's the link again: [link]. Please send it back by [date]."

If it still doesn't come back: proceed with the kickoff call anyway. Note at the start of the call that you didn't receive the form and spend the first few minutes collecting the answers verbally. Don't reschedule because of a missing form — that creates friction that's disproportionate to the stakes.

## What to do with the intake answers

Read the intake form the day before the kickoff call. Don't skim it on the way into the call.

As you read, write down:
- The one or two things in their answers that you want to explore further
- Any red flags about scope, expectations, or readiness
- The answer to question four (what success looks like) verbatim — you'll reference it on the call

At the start of the kickoff, reference the intake explicitly: "I read through your answers to the intake form — really helpful. I want to come back to what you said about [specific point] because I think there's something useful there." This tells the client that the form wasn't just admin overhead but was actually read and used.

## What a client intake form is not

An intake form is not a discovery questionnaire. A discovery questionnaire is what you send to a prospect before they've committed — it's part of the sales process. An intake form is for a client who has already signed and paid. The questions are different.

An intake form is not a comprehensive assessment. If your intake form requires 30 minutes to complete, you're asking too much. Five questions, clear and specific, is the ceiling.

An intake form is not a contract substitute. It doesn't establish scope, terms, or deliverables. It's background information that helps the first session be more useful.
