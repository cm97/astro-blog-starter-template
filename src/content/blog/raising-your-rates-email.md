You know your rate is too low. You've known for months. Every new project at the old price makes it harder to say.

Most people wait for the perfect moment, then send a long apology with three paragraphs of reasons. The client reads it as a question. A question invites a no.

A rate increase email is a notice, not a request. It's short, it names the new number and the date, and it says thank you. That's it.

## Before you write anything

Do these first. They take ten minutes.

1. **Work out the new number.** Don't guess. Take what you need to earn this year, add your expenses, and divide by the hours you can actually bill. That's your floor. If you're below it, you're paying to work.
2. **Pick the date.** Give existing clients 30 days' notice. Retainers change on the next billing cycle after that.
3. **Decide who gets it.** All active clients, same email, same date. Different deals for different people turn into a negotiation.
4. **Write down what doesn't change.** Same work, same turnaround, same way of working together. Clients worry about losing what they like.

Fill this in before you send:

```
Client: ________
Current rate: $________
New rate: $________
Starts on: ________ (at least 30 days out)
What stays the same: ________
Follow-up date: ________ (7 days after sending)
```

## The email

Subject: **Rates from [date]**

> Hi [first name],
>
> Thank you for the last [time working together]. Working on [project] has been a highlight.
>
> From [date], my rate for [type of work] will be $[new rate]. Everything else stays the same: [what stays the same].
>
> Anything already booked before [date] stays at the current rate.
>
> If you have questions, reply here and I'll answer them.
>
> [Your name]

Notice what isn't there. No "I'm sorry." No "I hope this is okay." No list of reasons. One thank you, one number, one date, one promise that the work stays the same.

## When they push back

Most clients reply "Thanks for letting me know." Some ask questions. Here's what to send.

**"Can you keep me at the old rate?"**

> I'm keeping everything booked before [date] at the current rate. From [date], it's $[new rate] for everyone. If the budget is tight, we could do [smaller version of the work] at that rate instead.

You offer a smaller scope, not a smaller price. The number holds.

**"Why the increase?"**

> My costs have gone up, and I want to keep giving [project] the time it needs. The new rate lets me do that.

One sentence. Don't write an essay.

**"We'll need to think about it."**

> Of course. Anything before [date] stays as it is. Let me know by [date a week before the change] so I can plan the schedule.

## If they go quiet

Send one follow-up after seven days.

> Hi [first name], just checking you saw my note about rates from [date]. Happy to answer any questions.

If you still hear nothing, the new rate starts on the date. Send the next invoice at the new rate with a one-line reminder.

## When to send it

- **Mid-week, mid-morning.** Not Friday afternoon, and not right after a problem on the project.
- **After a delivery they liked.** It's the easiest moment to say thank you and mean it.
- **At least 30 days before the change.** Less than that feels like a surprise.

## Don't make this a once-every-three-years panic

The hardest part of raising rates is the first time. After that it's a habit. Check your number once a quarter. If you're below your floor, send the email.
