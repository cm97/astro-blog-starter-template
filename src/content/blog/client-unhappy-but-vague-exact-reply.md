You send the draft. The reply comes back:

"Hmm. It's not quite there yet."

Or: "I'm not feeling it." Or: "Can you make it pop more?"

No list. No examples. You have no idea whether they want a small tweak or a full redo. So you guess, change three things, send it back, and get the same reply again. Each round costs you hours and makes the client a little less sure about you.

Vague feedback is rarely the client being difficult. Most people can tell something is off but don't have the words for why. Your job is to make answering easy.

## Don't redo anything yet

The worst move is to start changing things straight away. You'll change the wrong things, and now there are two versions to argue about.

Before you reply, write down three things:

```
Client: ________
What they said (exact words): ________
What I think they might mean (best guess): ________
What the brief / proposal asked for: ________
```

The last line matters most. Often "not quite right" means the work doesn't match something they never told you. Checking the brief tells you whether this is a fix or a change of direction.

## The reply

Send it the same day. Keep it short and easy to answer.

> Hi [name],
>
> Thanks for the honest reaction. I want to get this right, so I'd rather ask than guess.
>
> Could you help me with three quick things?
>
> 1. What's one part you like and want to keep?
> 2. What's the one part that feels most off?
> 3. Is there an example (a site, a video, a brand) that feels closer to what you have in mind?
>
> Short answers are perfect. Or if it's easier, we can do a 15-minute call and go through it together.
>
> [Your name]

## Why this works

- **"What do you like?" comes first.** It's the easiest question, so they start answering. It also tells you what not to touch.
- **"The one part" stops a vague list.** Asking for everything gets you "all of it". Asking for one part gets you something you can fix.
- **An example does the work words can't.** People who can't explain a feeling can usually point at it.
- **The call is a way out, not a demand.** Some clients find it easier to talk than write. Offering it shows you care about getting it right.

## When they answer with an example

Look at the example closely before you reply. Then repeat back what you'll change, in plain words:

> Thanks, that helps a lot. Here's what I'll change:
>
> - [Specific change, e.g. "brighter colours, closer to the example"]
> - [Specific change, e.g. "shorter intro, under 10 seconds"]
>
> I'll keep [the part they liked]. New version by [date].

Now you both agree on a short list before you do any work. If the next version misses, you can point at the list instead of starting again.

## When the answer is a new direction

Sometimes the example shows they want something different from what was agreed. That isn't a revision. It's a change of scope.

> Thanks, that's really useful. The example is a different style from the brief we agreed ([short description]), so it'd be a new direction rather than a round of changes.
>
> I can quote it as a change, or we can bring the current version closer to the example within the original brief. Which would you prefer?

## When they still can't say

If the call or the three questions still get "I'll know it when I see it", offer two small options instead of one big redo:

> No problem. I'll send two quick variations of [the part that feels off] by [date]. Just tell me which one is closer.

Choosing is easier than describing. Keep each variation small, so you're not doing two full projects.

## Stop it at the next delivery

Most vague feedback starts with a vague ask. When you send work, ask for feedback in one specific way:

> Could you send feedback by [date]? One list of changes in one reply helps me turn it around fastest.

And add one question:

> What's the one thing you'd change first?

You'll still get "not quite right" sometimes. You'll get it less, and you'll get it with something to work from.
