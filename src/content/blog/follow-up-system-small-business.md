Here's the follow-up problem in one sentence: you're relying on memory, and memory doesn't work reliably under pressure.

Most small business owners miss follow-ups not because they're careless. They miss them because they stored the reminder in their head at a moment when their head was full of something else. By the time they surface, the moment has passed.

The fix is simple but requires actually building it: get the reminder out of your head and into a system that shows it to you at the right time.

## What "falling through" actually looks like

The follow-up version of this usually looks like one of these:

- You send a proposal and mean to check in after five days. You get busy. Three weeks later you remember. The client went with someone else.
- You have a call with a lead and they say "reach out in a couple weeks." You have every intention of doing this. You don't.
- A client goes quiet mid-project. You notice, mean to send a check-in, and then a fire drill takes the rest of the day. You don't come back to it.

None of these are laziness. They're all the same problem: the follow-up existed as a mental note in a brain with no reliable retrieval system for mental notes.

## The follow-up system that actually works

There are three components:

**1. A capture habit**  
Every time you end a call, send a proposal, or have any interaction that should be followed up on, you log the follow-up immediately. Not when you get back to your desk — immediately, while it's in front of you.

This means the follow-up log needs to be accessible wherever you are. A notes app on your phone is fine. A dedicated follow-up inbox in your task system is fine. What doesn't work is "I'll add it when I'm back at my laptop," because you won't.

**2. A single place where follow-ups live**  
If your follow-ups are scattered across email flags, sticky notes, a notes app, a spreadsheet, and your task system, you'll check all of them inconsistently and miss things in whichever one you checked least recently.

Pick one place. Everything goes there. You check it at the start of every day.

**3. A consistent review**  
The follow-up system only works if you actually look at it. That means building a daily check-in — ideally 5 minutes at the start of the workday — where you look at what's due and decide what to send.

Not "I'll check it when I remember." A fixed time, every day.

## What a follow-up entry looks like

The simpler the entry, the more likely you are to create it in the moment.

At minimum, log:
- Who it's for
- What the follow-up is about
- When it should go out

That's it. You don't need notes on the full conversation — you need enough to write the follow-up when the time comes. If you need to re-read the thread to remember context, the entry should have a link to it. But don't let the need for a perfect entry stop you from logging a basic one.

## The three follow-up types you should have templates for

**The check-in after a proposal**  
Short. Direct. Asks one question. Doesn't apologize for following up. Goes out five to seven days after the proposal, unless you agreed otherwise.

**The mid-project check-in**  
A quick note to a client during a project. "Just checking in — how's everything looking on your end?" It builds trust, catches problems early, and takes two minutes.

**The re-engagement**  
For leads who went quiet. Short, no pressure, acknowledges the silence, opens the door. If there's no response after two of these, you mark the lead closed and stop.

Having these pre-written means you're never staring at a blank email when you need to follow up. You're filling in a name and hitting send.
