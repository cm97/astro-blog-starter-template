A retainer starts strong. Then week three hits and you have not sent an update. The client assumes the work is stuck or, worse, that you forgot them. They go quiet. You spend Thursday night drafting a novel to re-engage. That draft dies in drafts. Next week the same thing happens.

The failure mode is simple: the status lives in your head. When the week is slammed, the email never leaves.

You do not need a better memory. You need a fixed template you can fill in under five minutes.

## The 6-line weekly status email

Send this every Friday (or the same weekday every week). Same subject line. Same order. Copy, fill the blanks, send.

**Subject:** Weekly update — [project name]

Hi [Name],

Done this week:
- [one concrete thing finished]
- [second thing, if any]

In progress:
- [what is mid-flight and the date it lands]

Need from you by [day]:
- [one specific item, file, decision, or approval]

Next week:
- [the single next deliverable]

Any questions, reply to this thread.

[Your name]

That is the whole email. No project-management software required. No 400-word narrative.

## Why this exact shape

1. **Done** shows progress without asking them to hunt for it.
2. **In progress** sets the expectation so they do not chase you.
3. **Need from you** creates a clear next action on their side. Silence becomes their problem, not yours.
4. **Next week** keeps the retainer moving instead of restarting every Monday.
5. The fixed subject and order mean you never rebuild the structure.

If you have nothing new to report, still send it. Write “No new deliverables this week — waiting on [their item]. Next week: [X].” Silence is what kills the relationship.

## The 4-minute Friday routine

1. Open the last status email you sent this client (or the blank template).
2. Fill the four blanks from whatever you actually did. Do not invent.
3. Check the “need from you” line is one item only.
4. Send. Close the tab.

If you manage more than three active clients, do them in one sitting. Same template, different names.

## What still breaks when this only lives in your head

You skip a week because delivery ran long. The client notices the gap and starts wondering if the work is on track. You then write a longer apology email that still does not give them a clear next step. The cycle repeats until the retainer ends or they quietly stop replying.

A written checklist removes the decision. You either run the five minutes or you do not. There is no third option of “I will remember later.”
