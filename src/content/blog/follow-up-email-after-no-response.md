You sent the proposal. Or you finished the project. Or you had a great call and said you'd follow up. And then: nothing.

The follow-up email is the most consistently dreaded piece of business writing. Not because it's technically hard, but because it feels awkward — like you're being pushy, or admitting that you care more than they do, or starting a conversation that might end with a no.

So the email doesn't get written. Or it gets written and then rewritten into something so vague it might as well be nothing. "Just wanted to check in!" is not a follow-up. It's a way of following up that lets you pretend you didn't really try.

Here are five follow-up templates that work because they're direct, they're short, and they ask for something specific.

## What makes a follow-up email actually work

Before the templates: a follow-up email that gets a reply does three things.

**It references something specific.** Not "following up on our conversation" — "following up on the proposal I sent Tuesday for the [project name]." Specificity signals that this isn't a mass message and that you actually remember what you talked about.

**It asks one clear question.** The reason most follow-ups don't get replies is that they don't make it obvious what the person should do next. "Wanted to check in" requires the reader to decide what to do with that. "Is this still something you want to move forward with, or should I take it off my plate?" makes it easy to respond.

**It gives them an easy out.** The fear of looking pushy makes people write follow-ups that hedge so much the point disappears. Paradoxically, giving someone a clear "it's okay to say no" makes them more likely to engage. People respond to easy.

## Template 1: Proposal follow-up (5–7 days after sending)

Use when: You sent a proposal or quote and haven't heard back.

> **Subject:** Re: [Project name] proposal
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Following up on the proposal I sent [day] for [project name].
>
> Is this still something you want to move forward with, or do you need any changes before deciding?
>
> Happy to adjust the scope or timeline if anything has changed on your end.
>
> [Your name]

Why it works: It's four sentences. It restates what it's about. It asks one question. It offers flexibility without being needy. The subject line starts with "Re:" — not because it's a reply, but because "Re:" in a subject line reads as a continuation of a conversation, which it is.

**Send on a Tuesday or Wednesday morning.** Response rates on follow-up emails drop significantly on Fridays and Mondays.

## Template 2: Mid-project check-in

Use when: You're in the middle of an ongoing engagement and haven't heard from the client in a while.

> **Subject:** Quick check-in — [project name]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Just checking in on [project name] — we're at [milestone/week/stage] and things are on track on my end.
>
> Anything you want to flag before [next milestone]? Or anything you need from me right now?
>
> [Your name]

Why it works: It leads with reassurance (things are on track on my end), not a request. The two questions are answerable with a sentence each. This email takes 20 seconds to read and 30 seconds to reply to — and that's the goal.

**Send before problems develop, not after.** A mid-project check-in sent at week 3 of a 6-week engagement is maintenance. The same email sent when something has gone wrong is damage control. The maintenance version is easier to write and easier to receive.

## Template 3: Re-engagement for a quiet lead

Use when: You had a good initial conversation months ago but nothing came of it, and you want to check if things have changed.

> **Subject:** Checking back in — [their name or company]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> We talked back in [month] about [what you discussed]. Things moved fast on my end so I didn't follow up the way I should have.
>
> I know timing wasn't right then. Is [the problem you discussed] still something you're working on, or has it sorted itself out?
>
> Either way, no pressure — just wanted to check in.
>
> [Your name]

Why it works: It takes the blame for the silence (even if it wasn't your fault — it doesn't matter). It references what you actually talked about. It frames the question around their problem, not around your need for a sale. And "no pressure" at the end is real, not performative — you're genuinely giving them an easy exit.

**Don't apologize if you did nothing wrong.** "I'm sorry to bother you" is unnecessary and makes you sound smaller. "I know timing wasn't right then" acknowledges reality without groveling.

## Template 4: Referral request

Use when: A project went well and you want to ask if they know anyone who might need the same thing.

> **Subject:** One quick ask
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Really enjoyed working on [project] together. Nice to finish something that actually [result — e.g., "cut your onboarding time in half"].
>
> One quick ask: do you know anyone else who's dealing with [the problem you solved]? Even a name and a "might be worth a conversation" email intro is incredibly helpful.
>
> No worries if no one comes to mind. Just wanted to ask while the project is fresh.
>
> [Your name]

Why it works: It leads with a specific reference to a result, which reminds them why they're happy with you. The ask is framed as minimal effort ("even a name"). And it closes with a genuine out: "No worries if no one comes to mind."

**Ask for referrals at the right moment.** The window is roughly two to four weeks after a project ends — after you've delivered results but before the engagement has faded from memory. Earlier feels like you're asking for a favor before you've fully delivered. Later and they've moved on.

## Template 5: End-of-project wrap-up

Use when: A project has ended and you want to close it properly, leave a good impression, and keep the door open.

> **Subject:** [Project name] — wrapped up
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> [Project name] is wrapped up from my end.
>
> [One to two sentences: what was accomplished. Be specific. "The new onboarding system is live and your team has the guide to run it." Not "It was great working together."]
>
> I'll send the final deliverables/invoice/[whatever applies] separately. Let me know if anything needs to be adjusted.
>
> If you run into anything related to this down the road, feel free to reach out. And if you ever want to work together again on [area], I'd be glad to.
>
> [Your name]

Why it works: It confirms the work is done (which matters — some clients worry about invoices or lingering obligations). It restates the result in concrete terms. It opens the door to future work without being salesy. And it's short enough that they'll actually read it.

## The follow-up that doesn't get sent

The most expensive follow-up is the one that doesn't happen.

A proposal you sent and never followed up on has a conversion rate of near zero. The same proposal followed up on once, specifically and briefly, converts at a meaningfully higher rate — industry data consistently puts it at two to three times higher, depending on the business.

Most service providers don't follow up because writing the email feels uncomfortable. But the discomfort is in the anticipation, not the doing. Once you have templates — actual sentences you know work — the follow-up email takes three minutes and happens on time, every time.
