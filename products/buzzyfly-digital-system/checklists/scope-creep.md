# Scope Creep Checklist
### What to do when clients ask for more than you agreed to.

---

## The core principle

Scope creep is not a client problem — it's a systems problem. Clients ask for more because the original agreement wasn't specific enough, or because you let the first request slide without a response.

The fix is not to be difficult. It's to be clear, fast, and give them a path forward.

---

## STEP 1 — Recognize it early

Scope creep comes in four forms:

| Type | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| The add-on | "While you're at it, can you also..." |
| The revision loop | "Actually, can we try it a different way?" (after approval) |
| The moving target | "We decided to change the direction..." |
| The assumption | They expected something you never agreed to provide |

- [ ] Review the original contract or agreement if you're unsure
- [ ] If it's not in the agreement, it's out of scope — even if it seems small
- [ ] Small requests add up. One hour here, one hour there = a free day of work every month.

---

## STEP 2 — Respond immediately (use the scope guard script)

Do not ignore it, delay it, or quietly absorb it. Respond the same day.

**The scope guard (say this or write it):**
> "That's a great idea, and I want to make sure we do it right. It's outside what we agreed on for this phase, so here's what I'd suggest:
>
> Option 1: We add it as a separate piece of work — I can put together a quick quote.
> Option 2: We keep the current scope as-is, and you can decide about this later once you see how the first phase lands.
>
> Which feels better to you?"

- [ ] Always give two options — both of which respect your time
- [ ] Never say "that's not in scope" without offering a path forward
- [ ] Never absorb it silently — it sets a precedent you can't undo

---

## STEP 3 — Document the agreement

- [ ] If they choose to add it: get it in writing before you start
- [ ] Create a mini scope document: what's being added, at what price, by what date
- [ ] Send it by email so there's a paper trail (not just Slack or text)
- [ ] Update the original project notes with the change

**The add-on confirmation email:**
> Hi [Name],
>
> To confirm what we discussed: I'll be adding [specific deliverable] for an additional $[amount], to be completed by [date].
>
> This is separate from the original agreement. I'll invoice you for this separately.
>
> If that works, just reply "confirmed" and I'll get it scheduled.
>
> [Your name]

---

## STEP 4 — Revision policy (for deliverable-based work)

Define your revision policy in the original contract. Typical standard:

- **2 rounds of revisions included**
- **Additional revisions billed at your hourly rate**
- **Major redirects (changing the brief after approval) = new scope**

- [ ] If you haven't defined your revision policy in your contracts, do it now
- [ ] After round 2, say: "We've hit the revision limit on this. I can do one more round at $[rate]/hour — want to go ahead?"

---

## STEP 5 — Red flags that signal a scope problem is coming

Watch for these at the start of a project:

- [ ] Client references other work you've done as a bar ("I love how you did X for Y — can we do that here?")
- [ ] Multiple decision-makers with no single point of contact
- [ ] "We'll know it when we see it" as the success criteria
- [ ] Approval process is unclear ("I'll show it to my partner")
- [ ] Timeline was set without your input

If you see 2+ of these, tighten the scope document before you start.

---

## The rule

One absorbed scope request creates the expectation of another. The second one trains the client. By the third, they believe it's how you work.

Stop it at the first one. Kindly. Clearly. With a path forward.
